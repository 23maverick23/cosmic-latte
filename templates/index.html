Cosmic Latte

A light and dark color scheme.

The color of the universe

In 2002, Johns Hopkins astronomers Karl Glazebrook and Ivan Baldry described the combined visible light of galaxies as a pale, almost-white beige. After inviting name suggestions, they chose Cosmic Latte. Read the astronomers’ account.

The team used the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, a survey of roughly 200,000 galaxies. Its instrument on the Anglo-Australian Telescope in Australia could collect light from up to 400 objects at once, splitting it into spectra: records of how much light arrives at each wavelength. About the survey.

How do you turn all that starlight into one color?

Combining the light. The researchers corrected the spectra for redshift, the stretching of light toward red as the universe expands. They combined them into a luminosity-weighted “cosmic spectrum,” so galaxies contributed in proportion to the light they emitted. They then used standard models of human color vision to work out how that blend would look. Baldry explains the method.

From turquoise to beige. The first announcement, in January 2002, described a pale turquoise. Color scientist Mark Fairchild and colleagues helped trace that result to an incorrect white-point setting in the conversion software. White point describes what a viewer treats as white under a given light. With that setting corrected for the chosen viewing conditions, the result became beige. The March 2002 correction.

Studying the history of stars. The researchers compared features in the spectrum with models of stellar populations to study when stars formed. A population with more hot, young stars gives a different spectrum from one with more cool, older stars. Calculating a single color was a side project. Read the research paper.

This average describes visible light escaping from galaxies, with its brightness raised for display. It does not describe how bright the night sky looks. The screen color also depends on assumptions about vision, lighting, and the display; a hex code is a representation, not a universal physical constant.
